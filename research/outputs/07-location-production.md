# Three Friends, Three Places, One Show: An On-Location Production Guide

*Prepared for Carolyn, 2026-10-07. Prices are approximate US street prices from September–October 2026 listings and change often. "Verified" means I read the policy text, either directly or through its indexed text where the page blocked automated fetching. "Unverified" means you should confirm it before relying on it. This is practical guidance, not legal advice.*

**How this builds on earlier research:**
- `01-sample-analysis.md` set the look: cosy, lived-in rooms, visible mics, and Y2K, collage and CRT graphics.
- `03-production-and-tools.md` covered studio and dorm audio, editing software, loudness targets, hosting and the basic guest release.

This guide doesn't repeat those. It covers what changes when you **leave the room**: three interviews in three different places, joined by mixed-media bumpers, that still need to feel like one show.

---

## TL;DR

1. **Put a wireless lav on each person and record a second, independent backup.** A 2-person kit such as the DJI Mic Mini (about $169) or the DJI Mic 3 (about $219–259, which records its own backup internally) is the single best purchase. The audio from the room is what will make the three places sound different, and it's the hardest thing to fix later.
2. **Lock everything on camera.** Set the same phone, the same 1x lens, the same frame rate, white balance locked at 5600K, the same camera height and the same framing at every location. Consistency comes from repeating a checklist, not from luck.
3. **Two phones beat one camera.** Shoot a wide two-shot plus a tighter single on whoever is talking. Sync everything in DaVinci Resolve with a hand clap and waveform auto-sync.
4. **Get permission before you go.** On Harvard property, filming for public release needs advance permission (verified). Cafés need the owner's OK, ideally in writing. Cambridge asks students to file a filming request **two weeks ahead** for public-space shoots (verified). Massachusetts requires **everyone's consent to audio recording**, so say it on tape.
5. **Each location gets the same roughly 3-hour block:** set up, test, record, 60 s of room tone, 10 minutes of bumper B-roll, wrap. Back up the files the same night using one naming scheme.

## 0. Choosing the three locations

Pick the places for **sound first, look second**. A beautiful but loud location costs more in the edit than it gives you on screen. Choose three places that each match one strand of your references, so the variety looks intentional:

| Slot | Reference vibe (from 01) | Example spot | Main audio risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Indoor, private** | Emma Chamberlain bedroom, the poster-covered dorm on your board | A friend's room or a House common room | Echo from bare walls, radiators, hallway noise, roommates |
| **Semi-public** | *Happy Hour with Ina Garten*: drinks at a table | A café at a quiet hour, or a friend's kitchen | Espresso machine, music, other tables talking |
| **Outdoors** | SubwayTakes, the folding table in a field on your board | A courtyard, the riverbank, a quiet park bench | Wind, traffic, sirens, planes, passers-by |

**Scout every spot in person at the same time of day and on the same weekday you plan to shoot.** Stand there for 5 minutes with headphones on and your phone recording. Check:

- **Noise:** HVAC, fridges, music you can't control, construction schedules and nearby bells.
- **Light:** where the windows face and where the sun will be.
- **Practicalities:** whether there's a power outlet, and who you need to ask for permission.

## 1. Location audio

### 1.1 Picking quiet spots

- **Indoors:** soft rooms (rugs, curtains, beds, bookshelves) beat big hard rooms. Sit people **away from walls** and turn off fans, AC and fridges. Leave a note on the fridge so you remember to turn it back on. Close the windows.
- **Cafés:** shoot at opening time or in a mid-afternoon lull. **Ask the staff to pause the music.** If they can't, sit as far from the speakers and the espresso machine as you can. Music is the worst background sound because you can't edit around it, and it brings copyright problems if it's audible.
- **Outdoors:** put a building or hedge between you and the road. Avoid open fields on windy days, check the forecast (under about 10 mph is comfortable), and morning is usually calmer. Keep your backs to the noise source and your faces to the camera.

### 1.2 Lav vs handheld vs on-camera mic

| Mic type | Pros | Cons | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wireless lavalier** (clip-on transmitter) | Close to the mouth wherever people move. Invisible or neat on screen. Consistent sound across very different rooms. | Clothing rustle, batteries, wireless dropouts | **Your default for all three locations.** It's the best tool for making three rooms sound alike. |
| **Handheld dynamic** (e.g. Samson Q2U from guide 03) | Rejects background noise very well. Looks deliberately podcast-y, like the mics in your Pinterest shots. | Needs stands or hands, plus a recorder or interface. People forget to talk into it. | A seated table setup indoors, or a vox-pop style intro outdoors |
| **On-camera / phone mic** | Already there | Far from mouths, so it picks up the room and sounds different in every location | **A scratch track for syncing only.** Never your main audio. |

**Recommendation:** use wireless lavs on both of you. If you love the visible-mic look from your references, put a handheld or dynamic mic on the table as a **prop and backup track**, but cut from the lavs.

**Lav placement.** Clip the lav about a hand's width (6–8 in) below the chin, centred on the chest ([Rode – documentary sound tips](https://rode.com/en-au/about/news-info/three-tips-for-recording-great-documentary-sound)). Keep it clear of necklaces, hoodie strings and scarves. Smooth fabrics take adhesive mounts well; textured fabrics are better with clips ([AnyHelpNow – clean dialogue](https://anyhelpnow.com/blog/clean-dialogue-recording)). Tell friends in advance: **no noisy jewellery, no crinkly jackets, and solid colours rather than tight stripes** (stripes shimmer on phone video).

### 1.4 Always record a backup track

Every segment should exist in **at least two independent recordings**:

1. **The main audio:** the lav kit, recorded internally or by the receiver into camera A or the phone.
2. **The backup:** a recorder on the table, internal transmitter recording, or a spare phone's Voice Memos.

Wireless dropouts, a dead battery or a receiver unplugged by accident happen to everyone. With a backup, it's a one-minute fix in the edit instead of a lost interview.

### 1.5 Room tone

Before you wrap at each location, everyone stays still and silent for **60 seconds** while all mics roll. You'll use it to cover cuts and fill gaps, and as a noise profile for cleanup. Thirty seconds is the minimum, and 30–60 seconds is the usual advice ([Syracuse – Field Sound Recording](https://nhtraining.syr.edu/field-sound-recording/)). Say "room tone, location 2" out loud first so you can find it later.

### 1.6 Wind outdoors

- Put the **furry windscreens ("dead cats")** that come with most kits on the transmitters. They're essential outdoors and optional indoors.
- Clip the lav **under a jacket's outer layer** or on the side away from the wind.
- Turn your backs to the wind; your bodies block it.
- If the wind roars in your headphones even with windscreens on, **move**. Wind rumble is very hard to remove later.

### 1.7 Monitoring with headphones

- Plug **wired** earbuds or closed-backs (the ATH-M40x from guide 03) into the receiver or camera and **listen during the test** and for the first few minutes of recording.
- Listen for hum, wind, rustle, distortion, wireless crackle and background music ([Syracuse](https://nhtraining.syr.edu/field-sound-recording/)).
- Set levels so normal talking peaks at about **-12 dBFS** and big laughs stay under **-6**. With 32-bit float this matters less, but set them sensibly anyway.
- Glance at the receiver's signal and battery indicators **every 10–15 minutes**. A friend or a "producer" friend can do this job.

## 2. Video

### 2.1 Phone vs camera

**Use phones.** A recent iPhone (or a good Android phone) at 1x is sharp, handles mixed light well, and has the lived-in, creator-made look your references have. A mirrorless camera only helps if you already own or can borrow one *and* know how to use manual exposure. Whatever you use, **use the same device and the same lens for camera A at every location**. That's the biggest single source of visual consistency.

**Shoot with the [Blackmagic Camera app](https://www.blackmagicdesign.com/au/products/blackmagiccamera/techspecs) (free)** rather than the native camera. It lets you lock:

- **White balance:** 5600K for daylight, 3200K for warm bulbs. Turn on "Lock White Balance on Record."
- **Shutter:** 180°, which gives natural motion blur.
- **ISO, focus and frame rate.**

Leaving these on auto is what makes every location look different ([The App Whisperer – Blackmagic Camera settings 2026](https://theappwhisperer.com/2026/07/blackmagic-camera-app-settings-explained-2026/)).

**Recommended show settings:**
- 4K or 1080p
- **24 fps** for a cinematic look, or **30 fps** for a vlog-ish one (pick one forever)
- 180° shutter
- WB locked
- Exposure and focus locked on faces
- Apple Log **off** unless you're comfortable colour grading. The standard profile is easier to match.

### 2.2 One camera vs two

| Setup | What you get | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **One phone, wide two-shot** | Simple, but cutting out "ums" leaves visible jump cuts | Acceptable. Hide the cuts with zoom-ins (crop 4K to about 120%) and with your collage bumpers. |
| **Two phones: A = wide two-shot, B = tighter shot on the guest** | You can cut between angles to hide edits, and the guest's reactions read clearly | **Recommended.** Camera B can be an old phone or a friend's. |
| **Three phones: wide plus a single on each person** | The classic podcast "multicam" | Nice, but it triples the files. Save it for if it becomes a series. |

Hiding edits matters a lot in a three-segment episode: you'll be trimming 30–60-minute conversations down to roughly 8–15 minutes each.

### 2.3 Framing a two-person conversation

- **Seat people at about 90° to each other** (corner of a table, two ends of a couch) rather than side by side or straight across. Both faces stay visible to camera A, and it feels intimate, like Ina's table setup.
- **Camera A (wide):** both people, framed from about mid-thigh or the table edge up, at **eye height**. Leave some of the location visible (posters, café counter, trees); that's what makes each place distinct.
- **Camera B (single):** the guest from roughly the chest up, placed **near your shoulder**, so the guest looks *just off* the lens, not into it.
- **Keep both cameras on the same side of the line between you** (the 180° rule), or the eyelines will flip when you cut.
- Keep **your seating side the same at every location**, for example you on the left and the guest on the right. It's a cheap, invisible consistency trick.
- Shoot **horizontal (16:9)** for YouTube. Keep the main action in the centre third so you can crop vertical 9:16 clips later (see guide 04 on social clips).

### 2.4 Lighting with a window or a cheap LED

- **A window is your key light.** Face the guest *toward* or at 45° to the window, never with their back to it (that gives you a silhouette). Overcast days give soft, even light, which is ideal.
- **Don't mix colours.** If you use daylight, turn off warm ceiling bulbs or swap them for daylight bulbs, and set the LED to 5600K. Mixed light is the main reason footage looks "off" and won't match.
- **Cheap LEDs:**
  - [NEEWER Basics two-pack bi-colour panels](https://plastiquealaloupe.fondationtaraocean.org/products/neewer-basics-2-pack-led-video-light-panel-lighting-kit-bi-c/212169773/), about $40
  - A [NEEWER 13" panel with stand](https://plastiquealaloupe.fondationtaraocean.org/products/neewer-13-led-video-light-panel-studio-lighting-kit-with-tri/209189784/), about $65
  - A [Lume Cube 2.0](https://hotrodcameras.com/products/lume-cube-2-0-daylight-balanced-portable-led-light-black-single), about $90 (portable, daylight only)

  Bounce the light off a white wall, or point it through a white shower curtain or parchment paper to soften it.
- **Cosy look:** your references are warm and lived-in. Get that with **a practical lamp in the background** (a fairy light, a desk lamp), not by warming the camera's white balance.
- **Outdoors:** put the sun **behind or beside** your subjects, not in their faces (squinting), and shoot in open shade or during golden hour. Midday sun is harsh.

### 2.5 Keeping a consistent look across very different places

What should be **the same** everywhere:
- the camera and lens
- the camera height (eye level)
- the framing sizes
- white balance and frame rate
- your seating side
- the mic placement
- one recurring **"set piece"** in frame

For the set piece, pick something on-brand from your Pinterest boards: a small CRT TV or retro monitor, a stack of zines, a particular mug, a fabric throw, or a visible branded mic flag. Bring it to every shoot.

What can **change**: the backgrounds, the light quality and the outfits. That variety is the point of the format.

## 3. Consistency: the house style

### 3.1 House-style checklist (print it and tape it inside the gear bag)

**Before rolling**
- [ ] Camera A: same phone and lens (1x), on a tripod at **eye height**, wide two-shot, me on the **left**
- [ ] Camera B: guest single, near my shoulder, same side of the line
- [ ] Blackmagic Camera: **[24 / 30] fps**, 180° shutter, **WB locked** (5600K daylight / 3200K tungsten), focus and exposure locked on faces
- [ ] Both phones on Airplane Mode + Do Not Disturb, with 50 GB+ free and battery over 80% (or plugged in)
- [ ] Lavs: a hand-width below the chin, centred, with nothing rubbing; windscreens on if outdoors
- [ ] Levels: normal talk around -12 dBFS, laughs under -6; checked in headphones
- [ ] Backup recorder rolling and placed on the table
- [ ] Set piece in frame
- [ ] One light source colour only (all daylight or all warm)

**On tape, in this order**
1. **Slate:** "Episode 1, segment 2, café, take 1" + **one loud clap** visible on both cameras
2. **Consent line:** "We're recording audio and video for [show name], is that okay?" with the guest's spoken "yes"
3. **The fixed intro line**, the same words every time: *"This is [show name]. I'm Carolyn, I'm at [place], and I'm here with [friend]."*
4. The interview
5. **The fixed outro line**, e.g. *"That was [friend] at [place]. Next stop..."* (it leads into the bumper)
6. **Room tone:** 60 seconds of silence, labelled out loud

**Before wrapping**
- [ ] 10 minutes of B-roll for the bumpers (see 3.3)
- [ ] Photo of the setup (helps you match the next location)
- [ ] Release and location permission confirmed

### 3.2 Matching sound and colour in the edit

**Sound.** Do these steps in order in DaVinci Resolve's Fairlight page, Audition or Descript.

1. **Sync and choose the best track** (lav vs backup) for each person.
2. **Remove noise per location:** use that location's room tone as the noise profile, or run Adobe Podcast Enhance or Descript Studio Sound *lightly* (guide 03). Use the same tool and a similar strength on all three segments, or one will sound "processed" next to the others.
3. **EQ to match:** a high-pass filter at about 80 Hz on all voices, then small cuts or boosts so the café and outdoor voices sound like the dorm voice. Audition's **Match Loudness** and Resolve's **dialogue leveler** help here.
4. **Fill cuts with room tone** so the background doesn't drop out between edits.
5. **Use one music bed and one sound signature** for all the bumpers. Bumpers are where the episode's sound becomes one show.
6. **Master the whole episode** to the targets in guide 03: -16 LUFS stereo, true peak at -1 dBTP or lower.

**Colour** (in Resolve's Color page; it's free):

1. **Correct each segment to neutral first.** Point the white-balance picker at something white or grey. Hold a sheet of white printer paper next to the guest's face for 2 seconds at each slate to give yourself a reference.
2. **Match the skin tones across segments.** Put a still of segment 1 beside segment 2 and use Resolve's split-screen to compare.
3. **Then apply one shared "show look"** to all three: a subtle warm, faded, slightly grainy grade that echoes the risograph and CRT palette of your boards. Save it as a LUT or a PowerGrade and reuse it every episode.
4. **Keep the heavy stylisation (CRT scanlines, collage, pink, green and yellow overlays) for the bumpers.** Keep the interviews natural so the stylised bumpers pop.

### 3.3 Shooting material for the bumpers at each location

Your bumpers mix media (collage, Y2K desktop UI, CRT, from 01). Collect raw ingredients at every shoot so each bumper is *about* the place you just left or are going to next:

- 5–10 still photos of the location: textures, signs, the guest's objects
- 15-second clips: the guest walking in, hands on a mug, the street, leaves
- A short audio "postcard": 20 s of the café hum, the birds, the dorm hallway
- Optional: film the guest saying one line straight to camera for the bumper ("next, we're going to...")

Later, layer them into Windows 95 or Paint frames, desktop-folder transitions or a CRT TV wall. The places become a visual thread instead of a mismatch.

### 4.2 Cambridge and Boston public places

- **Cambridge (verified):** the city asks anyone filming in Cambridge to submit its online **[Filming Request Form](https://cambridgema.gov/iwantto/filminthecityofcambridge/filmrequestform)**. For **"small film shoots by students, person on the street interviews, etc." it asks for two weeks' notice** (60 days for large commercial shoots). Most filming in the public right-of-way may also need a **Sidewalk Obstruction Permit**, and staff will tell you if it applies. Contact: Severo Rodriguez, 617-349-4743, srodriguez@cambridgema.gov ([City of Cambridge – Film in the City](https://www.cambridgema.gov/iwantto/filminthecityofcambridge)). Even a tiny seated interview on a public bench is safest with the form filed.
- **Boston (verified for formal permits):** go through the **Mayor's Office of Tourism, Sports and Entertainment** (tourism@boston.gov, 617-635-3911). A formal permit requires **$1M liability insurance and a $5,000 security bond**, and you should apply 10+ days ahead ([Boston.gov – How to apply to film in Boston](https://www.boston.gov/departments/tourism-sports-and-entertainment/how-apply-film-boston)). *Unverified:* the threshold below which a permit isn't needed. Third-party guides say a solo phone shooter usually doesn't need one, but tripods, lights and sound gear make one more likely ([Beverly Boy](https://beverlyboy.com/?p=130038)). **Because the insurance requirement is out of reach for a student, choose your outdoor location in Cambridge or on campus, not in Boston,** unless the city tells you in writing that a no-permit micro-shoot is fine.
- **Parks, the riverbank and the MBTA** may each belong to a different authority (state DCR land, the MBTA). *Unverified:* ask the Cambridge film contact which applies to your spot.

### 4.3 Cafés and other private businesses

- A café is **private property open to the public**: you need the **owner's or manager's permission**, ideally in writing ([Beverly Boy – filming in a restaurant](https://beverlyboy.com/filmmaking/how-to-get-permission-to-film-in-a-restaurant/)). Ask in person, offer to come at a quiet hour, buy something, and offer to tag the café.
- Get a **simple location release** (one paragraph, signed or emailed). It should name the café, the date, permission to film and to publish the footage, including in social clips ([FilmDaft – location release](https://filmdaft.com/what-is-a-location-release-form-definition-uses-free-template/)).
- Ask them to **pause the music**. That's also your copyright protection.

### 4.4 Massachusetts two-party (all-party) consent

- Massachusetts law (**M.G.L. c. 272 § 99**) makes it a crime to **secretly** record a conversation without **every party's consent**. A recording is not "secret" when everyone has actual knowledge of it. Penalties go up to $10,000 and 5 years ([Romano Law](https://romanolaw.com/?p=22079), [Boston Bar Journal](https://bostonbar.org/journal/secretly-recording-public-officials-challenges-to-the-massachusetts-wiretap-act/)).
- **For your guests:** visible mics, a signed release, plus the **spoken consent line on tape** covers it.
- **For bystanders' conversations:** the law covers *audio*, not images. Your gear is visible, so recording isn't secret, but **don't point mics at, or keep, strangers' intelligible conversations**. Use lavs on the two of you (they barely pick up neighbours), sit away from other tables, and cut or duck any stranger speech that's clearly audible. *(A cautious reading, not legal advice.)*

### 4.5 Release forms for your friends

Use the guest release checklist from guide 03, Section 7, and add these location-specific items:

- **Location, date and that it's part of a multi-location episode.** If it may become a series, write **"for the podcast [name], in any episode, season, trailer or promotional clip."**
- **Permission for B-roll and stills** of them to be used in **bumpers, collages, cover art and thumbnails**, including stylised or edited versions (your collage aesthetic changes how they look, so say so).
- **AI-assisted editing** (captions, enhancement, clips).
- **A review promise** if you choose to offer one (e.g. "you can flag anything you want cut within 7 days of seeing the rough cut").
- Signatures **before** recording. Keep a PDF or a Google Form response per guest in the project folder.

### 4.6 Bystanders in the background

- In truly public places, people incidentally in the background generally **don't need releases**, as long as the use isn't offensive or misleading. Anyone **featured or interviewed** should sign one ([Rocket Lawyer – filming in public](https://www.rocketlawyer.com/business-and-contracts/intellectual-property/releases-and-licensing/legal-guide/ask-a-lawyer-filming-in-a-public-space), [Stanford Fair Use – releases](https://fairuse.stanford.edu/overview/interview-and-property)).
- **Private places open to the public (cafés)** have higher expectations of privacy, and the owner may require you to protect customers ([Rocket Lawyer](https://www.rocketlawyer.com/business-and-contracts/intellectual-property/releases-and-licensing/legal-guide/ask-a-lawyer-filming-in-a-public-space)). **On campus, Harvard's rule requires explicit consent from any identifiable community member** (4.1).
- **Practical defaults:**
  - Frame so the background is a wall, a shelf or a window, not a room full of faces.
  - Use a wide aperture or Portrait/Cinematic mode on camera B for background blur.
  - In a café, put a **small sign** at the table or door ("Filming a student podcast here 2–4pm. Ask us if you'd rather not be in shot").
  - **Blur or crop out** any recognisable face you didn't get consent for, especially classmates.

## 6. Per-location shoot-day schedule (about 3 hours)

Times assume a 45–60 minute conversation that you'll cut down to an 8–15 minute segment.

| Time | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **-14 days** | Permissions | Harvard / Cambridge / café emails sent; release form sent to the guest |
| **-1 day** | Prep | Charge everything; clear phone storage; check the weather (outdoors); confirm with the guest (outfit guidance: solid colours, nothing noisy) |
| **0:00** | Arrive and scout | Listen with headphones for 2 minutes; turn off fans and fridges; ask for the music to be paused; pick seats (you on the left, at 90°) |
| **0:15** | Set up (about 35 min) | Tripods, framing, lights, white balance locked, the set piece placed; mic up the guest |
| **0:50** | Test (about 15 min) | Record 1 minute of chat; **play it back on headphones**; check both cameras' framing, focus and exposure; check the backup is rolling; fix things |
| **1:05** | Record (about 60 min) | Slate + clap → consent line → intro line → interview → outro line. Check batteries and levels every 15 minutes; take a quick break at about 30 minutes if needed |
| **2:05** | Room tone + bumper material (about 15 min) | 60 s of silence; stills, B-roll and the audio postcard; the guest's one-liner to camera |
| **2:20** | Wrap (about 20 min) | Photo of the setup; confirm the release is signed; reset furniture and **turn the fridge back on**; thank the location |
| **That evening** | Offload and back up | Copy everything (see Section 7); check every file plays; write a few notes on the best moments |

**For one episode:** spread the three shoots across 1–2 weeks so you can learn from shoot 1. If you do two in a day, allow at least 1.5 hours between them for travel, recharging and offloading.

**If it becomes a series:** batch-shoot two or three guests per weekend, keep one master gear setting, and do a quick "match check" against the previous episode's still before each shoot.

## 7. Managing files

### 7.1 Folder structure

```
ShowName/
  EP01_title-slug/
    00_admin/        releases, location permissions, outline
    01_raw/
      SEG1_dorm/
        camA/  camB/  audio_main/  audio_backup/  broll_stills/
      SEG2_cafe/ ...
      SEG3_outdoor/ ...
    02_project/      Resolve or Descript project files
    03_bumpers/      collage assets, graphics, music
    04_exports/      masters, clips, thumbnails
```

### 7.2 Naming convention

`YYYYMMDD_EP##_SEG#_LOCATION_SOURCE_###`

Examples:
- `20261018_EP01_SEG2_cafe_camA_001.mov`
- `20261018_EP01_SEG2_cafe_lavGUEST_001.wav`
- `20261018_EP01_SEG2_cafe_backup_001.m4a`
- `20261018_EP01_SEG2_cafe_roomtone.wav`

Rename at offload, not later. Put the date first so files sort chronologically. Never use spaces.

### 7.3 Backups (3-2-1)

- **3 copies:** the phone or recorder original, the laptop, and an external SSD or cloud.
- **2 types of storage:** local drive + cloud (iCloud / Google Drive, or Harvard's Google Drive / OneDrive if you have them).
- **1 off-site copy:** the cloud copy.
- **Don't delete anything from the phones or recorders until two copies are verified.** Open a random clip from each folder and check it plays.

At roughly 4K 30 fps, plan on **about 25–45 GB per camera per hour**. Free up phone storage before every shoot, or record in 1080p if space is tight.

### 7.4 Syncing audio to video

1. The **clap at every slate** gives a sharp spike on every track.
2. In **DaVinci Resolve (free):** select the video and audio clips on the Media page, right-click, choose **Auto Sync Audio → Based on Waveform**. For two cameras, create a **multicam clip** synced by sound ([MiraCamp – sync audio in Resolve](https://www.miracamp.com/learn/davinci-resolve/sync-audio-and-video), [DaVinci Resolve Club – multicam](https://davinciresolveclub.com/davinci-resolve-multicam-editing/)). Descript and Premiere also auto-sync by waveform.
3. If auto-sync fails, line up the clap spike by hand. If the clips **drift** over long takes (often a variable-frame-rate phone issue), re-sync at the halfway point. Keep each take under about 30 minutes, or use the Blackmagic app, which records a constant frame rate.
4. **Mute the camera audio** once synced and use the lav tracks, with the backup on a lower track ready to swap in.

## 8. If one episode becomes a series

| Area | One episode | Series |
|---|---|---|
| Gear | Borrow from Lamont/Cabot, plus one lav kit | Buy your own camera B and lights, so the look doesn't change with whatever is available to borrow |
| Permissions | A one-off request per location | Ask Harvard for **standing approval for the season**; file each Cambridge request two weeks ahead (one per shoot unless they tell you otherwise) |
| Releases | Per guest, for this episode | Release language covering "any episode, season and promotion"; keep a single tracker sheet |
| House style | A checklist | A one-page **style bible**: settings, framing stills, the grade LUT, intro and outro scripts, bumper template |
| Files | One project folder | Keep `EP##` numbering forever; archive finished episodes to cold storage |
| Locations | Three unique spots | Consider one **recurring "home base"** location plus two new ones each episode. It anchors the show visually, like Ina's apartment or SubwayTakes' subway. |
