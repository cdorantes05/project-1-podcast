# Episode Plan: Three Friends, Three Places, One Episode

Prepared for Carolyn.

This plan covers one video episode: three interviews with friends, each filmed somewhere different, joined by mixed-media bumpers. It builds on the earlier research files and doesn't repeat them:

- **01-sample-analysis.md** covers her references: cosy two-person shows, lyric videos, and the collage, Y2K and CRT boards.
- **02-format-and-craft.md** covers general interview craft, follow-up questions, a generic pre-interview checklist and a generic question bank.
- **03-production-and-tools.md** covers gear, the audio loudness target, consent law and release forms.
- **04-launch-plan.md** covers the launch timeline and clips.

When something here only matters if the show becomes a series, it is marked **[Series note]**.

---

## 0. The core design problem (read this first)

Every reference show in Carolyn's playlist keeps one thing fixed:

- **The setting:** Ina Garten's apartment, the SubwayTakes subway car, the chicken shop in Chicken Shop Date.
- **The host's persona.**

[Chicken Shop Date works](https://stanforddaily.com/2024/09/25/chicken-shop-date/) partly because every guest is put in "the same low-status environment". Fixing the setting is what makes the episodes feel like one show.

**Carolyn's concept changes the setting on purpose,** so something else has to stay fixed. In this plan, three things stay the same:

1. **The same core questions**, asked in the same order (Section 5).
2. **The same visual system:** the collage and Y2K desktop frames from 01, used for every bumper, lower third and title card.
3. **The same camera grammar:** identical framing, lens height and mic placement in all three locations.

If those three hold, the changing locations read as a feature rather than as three unrelated videos stitched together.

## 1. Four possible themes

Each theme has a one-line hook (the 01 takeaway was that every reference show has one), a shared question, and a rule for picking locations.

### Theme A: "Who were you here?" (place as a time machine)
- **Hook:** Three friends go back to a place that shaped them and talk about who they were then and who they are now.
- **Shared question:** "Who were you the first time you came here, and would that person like you now?"
- **How location helps:** Each friend picks a spot tied to a specific past version of themselves, for example:
  - the first-year dorm entryway
  - a hometown diner, or a stand-in for it
  - the practice room where they quit an instrument

  The place prompts specific memories, so the stories come out on their own.
- **Fits the board:** Strong match for the "Who Am I?" folders and the old-photos-with-doodles pins. Bumpers can use CRT and VHS-rewind textures.
- **Risk:** It can turn heavy. Balance it with the lighter core questions.

### Theme B: "Open folder" (each friend is a desktop folder)
- **Hook:** Carolyn's desktop has three folders, one per friend. Each episode "opens" a folder and goes inside.
- **Shared question:** "What's in the folder you'd never let anyone open?" Use it as a playful prompt, not a demand for secrets.
- **How location helps:** Each location is the friend's natural habitat: the room or place where they spend the most hours. The location *is* the folder's contents.
  - The art-studio friend gets the studio.
  - The friend who lives at the gym gets the gym.
  - The friend who studies in the same library carrel every night gets the carrel, if permission allows.
- **Fits the board:** This is the most direct use of the Mac-folders and Windows-95 pins. Bumpers are literally a cursor double-clicking the next folder.
- **Risk:** Lowest of the four. It is the **recommended default** because it is easy to explain, easy to make look good, and it extends to a series as new folders.

### Theme C: "Defend your spot" (a SubwayTakes-style premise)
- **Hook:** Each friend brings Carolyn to *their* favourite place in Cambridge or Boston and has to defend it as the best one.
- **Shared question:** "Make the case: why is this the best place in the city, and what does it say about you that you think so?"
- **How location helps:** The location is the argument. It also gives a built-in competitive thread: at the end, Carolyn or the audience in the comments picks a winner.
- **Fits the references:** Matches the "hot takes" energy of SubwayTakes, where every episode opens with "So, what's your take?" ([THR](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-features/kareem-rahma-interview-subway-takes-emmys-1236609132/)). It also gives the most clippable moments.
- **Risk:** Public places mean noise and permission problems (see the checklist in Section 3). Pick spots that stay quiet at a predictable hour.

### Theme D: "Past / present / future" (the three friends as three tenses)
- **Hook:** One question, asked three ways across three places.
  - Friend 1 is at a place from their past and answers about the past.
  - Friend 2 is at their daily place and answers about now.
  - Friend 3 is at a place they want to be, such as an empty lecture hall, an airport café or a gallery, and answers about the future.
- **Shared question:** "What are you becoming?" The phrasing shifts by tense: "What were you trying to become?", "What are you becoming right now?", "What do you hope you'll have become?"
- **How location helps:** The locations form a deliberate arc, so the episode has a shape (beginning, middle, end) without needing a script.
- **Fits the board:** Bumpers can move from CRT texture (past), to phone-screen frame (present), to a glitchy or blank new desktop (future).
- **Risk:** It is the hardest to keep comparable, because the questions shift. It also only works if the order of the friends is fixed.

**How to choose:** Pick **B** if the priority is a clean, repeatable format. Pick **D** if this is a one-off with an artistic arc. Pick **C** if the priority is short clips. **A** works best if all three friends share a place, such as all being from the same entryway.

**[Series note]:** B and C repeat indefinitely (new folders, new spots). A and D are better as a single special episode, or as a season-long arc with one tense per season.

## 2. Run of show (about 45 minutes)

The timings follow 02's guidance that a cold open lands in the first 45 seconds and the intro stays under 30 seconds. Each interview is about 12 minutes of *edited* time, which needs about 35 to 45 minutes of raw tape per friend. The total fits the 30 to 50 minute range from 01.

| Time | Segment | What happens | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:50 | **Cold open: "the same question, three answers"** | Three fast cuts, one per friend, each answering the *same* shared question in a different location. End on the most surprising answer. | This is the format's biggest advantage. No single-guest show can open with three contrasting answers. Use hard cuts, no music under the first line, and a sting on the last word. |
| 0:50–1:20 | **Intro / title** | Title card in the desktop or collage look. Carolyn on camera at her "home base" (bed or desk, from the 01 board) says the hook in one sentence and names the three places. | Keep it under 30 seconds. Use the same home-base shot in every episode. |
| 1:20–1:50 | **Bumper 0: the map** | The desktop shows three folders or icons with friend names and location names. The first one opens. | Sets expectations ("three of these") so viewers know how long the episode is. |
| 1:50–14:00 | **Interview 1** | Location arrival shot, then core questions 1 to 6, the friend-specific block, core questions 7 to 10, and the chain question. | Edited down from about 40 minutes of tape. |
| 14:00–15:00 | **Bumper A** | Recap, travel, reveal. See below. | 45 to 75 seconds. |
| 15:00–27:00 | **Interview 2** | Same structure. Opens by answering Friend 1's chain question. | |
| 27:00–28:00 | **Bumper B** | Contrast, travel, reveal. See below. | |
| 28:00–40:00 | **Interview 3** | Same structure. Opens by answering Friend 2's chain question. | |
| 40:00–42:30 | **The braid (finale)** | Two or three core questions intercut: all three friends answer each one back to back. | This pays off the cold open. Use the strongest *contrast* questions. |
| 42:30–44:30 | **Outro** | Carolyn back at home base, recorded *after* watching the edit. One-sentence takeaway on what the three answers add up to, then a thank-you to the friends by name. | Recorded later so she can react to the material (02 Template B advice). |
| 44:30–45:00 | **CTA and end card** | One ask, then an end card showing the three folders "closed" plus a tease for the next episode. | |

**A 25-minute version** (if the material is thin, or for a pilot): run the cold open, a 15-second intro, three 7-minute interviews, two 30-second bumpers and a 1.5-minute braid-plus-outro.

### What each bumper does narratively

Bumpers are 45 to 75 seconds long. Each one does **three jobs in order: look back, move, reveal.** Built from mixed media, a bumper has these layers:

1. **Look back (about 10 seconds):** one line from the friend who just finished, shown as a pull quote. It can appear as a Windows-95 dialog box, a hand-lettered caption over a photo, or text on a CRT. This is the "recap" the viewer takes with them.
2. **Move (about 20 to 30 seconds):** the journey. Use phone B-roll of walking or riding the T to the next place, sped up or stop-motion. Lay over it a collage of 5 to 8 stills (Polaroids of the next location, torn-paper map pieces, a Street View-style frame like the one on the board). Carolyn's voice-over is recorded afterward, in her own words, and gives one line of context about the next friend: "Maya and I met because we were both locked out of Canaday at 2 a.m." This is where the audience gets the backstory, so the interview itself carries fewer inside references (see Section 4).
3. **Reveal (about 10 to 15 seconds):** the next folder opens, and the next friend appears in frame at their location. A lower third gives their name and the location. Then a **question card** appears: the chain question the previous friend left for them.

Each bumper also has its own narrative job:

- **Bumper A (after Friend 1)** sets up the *pattern*: "now you'll see how someone else answers this." It should name the shared question again so viewers can compare.
- **Bumper B (after Friend 2)** names the *tension*: "Friend 1 said X. Friend 2 said the opposite. Friend 3 has to break the tie." By the middle of an episode viewers need a reason to stay for the third segment, and a disagreement is the strongest one.

**[Series note]:** Lock the bumper into a template: same length, same sting, same three beats. Then only the stills and the voice-over change each episode. That cuts editing time a lot and makes the bumpers into a recognisable brand element, the way the opening question is for SubwayTakes.

## 3. Matching friends to locations, and scouting

### Choosing the friends
- **Pick for contrast, not closeness.** Three friends who would answer the shared question differently make a better episode than your three closest friends. Map them quickly on two axes, such as introvert to extrovert and certain to searching, and avoid picking three people from the same corner.
- **At least one must be a confident talker.** Put them **first or last**. Interview 1 has to hook viewers, and Interview 3 has to land the ending. Put the quieter friend in the middle, where the bumpers and the chain question give them structure.
- **Ask each friend for their best story ahead of time** (Section 4). If they can't name one, either the theme doesn't suit them or they're the wrong guest for this episode.

### Matching each friend to a location
For each friend, check the place against these five tests:

1. **Does the place pull a story out of them?** A place where *something happened* beats a place that just looks good.
2. **Is it theirs, not yours?** The friend should be the expert there. That puts them at ease and makes Carolyn the curious outsider, which is the stance a good interviewer needs.
3. **Do the three places contrast?** Aim for a mix of indoor and outdoor, quiet and lively, day and evening, and different colour palettes. Three cafés will blur together.
4. **Can you control it for 60 minutes?** If you can't control the sound and keep people from walking through the shot for an hour, it's a B-roll location, not an interview location. Film 30 seconds of the beautiful-but-noisy spot for the bumper, then sit down somewhere controllable nearby.
5. **Does it fit the theme's rule?** For example: their habitat for B, their favourite spot for C, the matching tense for D.

**Worked example (Theme B, "Open folder"):**

| Friend | Their "folder" | Interview spot | B-roll / bumper material |
|---|---|---|---|
| Friend 1 (theatre kid, big talker) | The stage | An empty rehearsal room after hours (ask the student group) | Wings, costume rack, ghost light |
| Friend 2 (quiet, intense studier) | Their late-night routine | Their own dorm room at night, with desk lamp light | Coffee-cup collection, sticky notes, the walk to Lamont |
| Friend 3 (always outside) | The river | A bench on the Charles at golden hour (wind permitting; see checklist) | Rowers, bikes, the bridge |

### Permission (Harvard and Cambridge specifics)
This section is not legal advice. Confirm each case with the office named.

- **Harvard property:** The University's policy says anyone filming on Harvard property "for broadcast, public dissemination, or commercial usage" needs advance permission, starting with the communications office of the relevant School or unit. It also says filming students requires written consent ([Harvard Provost policy](https://provost.harvard.edu/photographing-and-filming-on-harvard-property)). There is a carve-out allowing photos and video taken on University property to be used "on personal, non-commercial social media accounts" without suggesting endorsement. Whether a podcast published on YouTube and Spotify counts as personal social media or as "public dissemination" is unclear, so **email first**.
- **Inside buildings:** For example, SEAS guidance says cameras and media recording equipment are not allowed inside FAS buildings except in faculty offices, or in Harvard Libraries with a media pass. It also asks filmmakers to get advance permission from students who will appear even in wide shots of the Yard ([SEAS policy](https://seas.harvard.edu/office-communications/media-resources/media-filming-and-photography-policies)). **Libraries and classrooms are the hardest locations to get approved, so treat them as a stretch goal.**
- **Your own and your friends' dorm rooms** are the easiest option. You still need the roommate's OK, and nothing identifiable of the roommate should be in frame.
- **House common spaces and dining halls:** ask the House administrator or building manager. Avoid meal times.
- **Cambridge public space:** The City asks for a Filming Request Form, with **two weeks' notice for small student shoots and person-on-the-street interviews**. A Sidewalk Obstruction Permit may also be needed if you set up equipment in the public right-of-way ([City of Cambridge](https://cambridgema.gov/iwantto/filminthecityofcambridge)). A handheld phone with a lav mic on a bench is low-impact, but the safe course is to file the form.
- **Cafés and shops:** ask the manager in person, and offer to film at their quietest hour and tag them. Get the yes in writing (a text or email is fine).
- **Background people:** anyone identifiable in a published video is a consent question. Frame so that passers-by stay out of focus or out of shot. 03 covers the recording-consent law for the interview itself.

### Location-scouting checklist
Visit each location **at the same time of day and day of week you'll film**, and stay for 15 minutes. Record a test on your phone, because a phone recording catches things your ear tunes out ([Rev](https://webflow.rev.com/blog/location-scouting-can-make-or-break-your-interview), [creativeLIVE / Abba Shapiro](https://phoenix.creativelive.com/class/how-to-shoot-and-edit-an-interview-abba-shapiro/lessons/location-scouting)).

**Sound**
- [ ] Stand still and listen for 60 seconds. Note HVAC cycling on and off, fridges, buzzing lights, clocks, elevators and plumbing.
- [ ] Note what's outside the space: road, construction, bells, the T, dining-hall noise, practice rooms.
- [ ] Record 30 seconds on your phone and listen back on headphones.
- [ ] Note hard surfaces that echo (tile, glass, bare walls). Look for soft furnishings nearby that you could sit near.
- [ ] Outdoors, check the wind. If there's any wind, you need a deadcat windscreen on the lav mic.
- [ ] Plan to record 30 seconds of **room tone** at the start and end of the shoot, to cover edits.
- [ ] Ask whether music is playing there. Café music is copyrighted, it can't be edited out, and it can trigger copyright claims. Ask staff to pause it.

**Light**
- [ ] Note which direction the window or sun is and where it will be *at shoot time*. The friend should face the light, not sit with it behind them.
- [ ] Check for mixed colour temperatures, such as warm lamps plus blue daylight. Turn one off, or accept the warm look, which suits the cosy references.
- [ ] Outdoors, check golden-hour timing for the shoot date. In October in Boston it's early, roughly 5:15 to 6:00 p.m.
- [ ] Watch for overhead fluorescents. They flatten faces and may flicker on camera.

**Permission** (see above)
- [ ] Find out who owns or controls the space and who can say yes. Get the name and the yes in writing.
- [ ] Check whether a Harvard or Cambridge request is needed, and the lead time (Cambridge: 2 weeks).
- [ ] Check whether anyone else (roommates, staff, regulars) will be in frame or in earshot.

**Background**
- [ ] Look for depth: the friend should be 1 to 2 metres or more from the wall behind them, which gives a softer background.
- [ ] Include personal, specific objects in frame (posters, books, gear), as the 01 references do.
- [ ] Remove logos, copyrighted artwork you'd feature prominently, screens showing other content, and private information (whiteboards, mail).
- [ ] Check that the background looks different from the other two locations.

**Power and logistics**
- [ ] Note where the outlets are, and whether you can run a cable safely without tripping anyone.
- [ ] Count batteries. Bring a power bank for each phone, spare lav batteries, and enough phone storage for about 45 minutes of 4K or 1080p, plus a backup.
- [ ] Note seats at a usable height for both people. Two people at the same eye level gives the best two-shot.
- [ ] Find somewhere to put bags off camera, a restroom, and somewhere to warm up if outdoors.
- [ ] Have a bad-weather backup for every outdoor spot, decided *before* the day.

**Foot traffic**
- [ ] Note peak and quiet hours. Ask staff which hour is quietest.
- [ ] Find a camera angle that faces *away* from the walkway.
- [ ] Consider a small "Recording, thank you!" sign. It works surprisingly well in common rooms.

**Comparability across the three locations** (specific to this format)
- [ ] Use the same framing plan everywhere: a wide two-shot, plus a single on the friend at about chest height.
- [ ] Use the same mic setup: the same lav or handheld model on both people.
- [ ] Keep each recording session about 45 minutes, so each one yields a similar amount of material.
- [ ] Shoot all three interviews **within about 7 to 10 days**, so Carolyn's hair, energy and the season don't jump between segments.

## 4. Interviewing friends specifically

02 already covers question design, follow-ups and general prep. This section covers what's *different* when the guest is a friend.

### Inside jokes the audience can't follow
Interviewing very close friends invites in-jokes and references to shared history that listeners can't follow ([Matchmaker.fm](https://amplify.matchmaker.fm/prepare-for-podcast-interview-tips/)). The useful distinction is *inside jokes with your guest* (alienating) versus *inside jokes with your audience* (rewarding, once viewers have earned them) ([The Audacity to Podcast](https://theaudacitytopodcast.com/insidejokes)).

Ways to handle it:

- **Play the stranger.** Carolyn's job is to ask on the audience's behalf. Say it out loud: "Pretend I don't know this story. Tell it from the start."
- **Use the "explain or cut" rule.** In the edit, any reference that needs more than one sentence of context gets either a one-line caption (a Windows-dialog-style "context:" pop-up, which is on-brand) or cut.
- **Move the backstory into the bumper.** The bumper voice-over tells viewers how Carolyn and the friend met, so the interview doesn't stop to explain it.
- **Introduce nicknames.** If you call them "Bean", say once why.
- **Ask "how did we meet?" early** (Core Q2). It hands the audience the relationship in 60 seconds, and friends' two versions of the story are often funny.
- **Ban "you remember when…"** as a question. Rephrase it as "Tell them about the time…".
- **[Series note]:** Once the show has regular viewers, a *recurring* bit, such as the chain question or a catchphrase, becomes an inside joke *with the audience*. That's the good kind.

### Getting past small talk
- **Do the catching up before you press record.** Have 10 minutes of real catch-up off-mic, then start recording on a substantive question. Don't open with "So how's your semester been?"
- **Open with the place.** Core Q1 ("Why here?") is concrete, easy and about something in front of them. It's the smoothest way into a real story.
- **Escalate disclosure gradually.** Arthur Aron's well-known closeness study used question sets that become steadily more personal. It found that closeness grows through "escalating, reciprocal, personal self-disclosure" ([A More Beautiful Question](https://amorebeautifulquestion.com/36-questions/), [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/love-and-the-brain-part-1-the-36-questions-revisited)). The core bank in Section 5 follows the same slope.
- **Disclose something yourself.** "Reciprocal" is the key word. When a friend hesitates, Carolyn offering her own answer briefly unlocks theirs. Keep it short so it doesn't become her episode.
- **Ask what you've never asked them.** Friends skip questions because they assume they already know the answer. Before each interview, write down three things you *assume* about this friend and ask about one of them.
- **Allow silence.** Friends rush to fill pauses for each other. Count to four before following up.

### Keeping the three segments comparable
- Ask all ten core questions, **in the same order and with the same wording**. Read them off the card for the shared ones, even if it feels stiff. Wording changes the answer, and the braid finale depends on clean comparisons.
- Give each segment about the same raw recording time (about 40 minutes) and about the same edited time (about 12 minutes). If one friend is clearly stronger, give them 2 to 3 extra minutes, not 10.
- Use the same opener (Q1), the same closer (the chain question) and the same camera grammar.
- Ask the shared theme question **in the same slot** each time: around minute 20 to 25 of the recording, when they're warmed up but not tired.
- Keep a scorecard while editing (Section 6): which questions each friend gave a strong answer to. Aim for each friend to "win" at least one question in the braid.

### The chain question (a device built for this format)
At the end of each interview, ask the friend to **write a question for the next friend** on an index card, without telling them who that is. The next friend answers it on camera at the start of their segment. Friend 3 writes one for Carolyn, which she answers in the outro.

The chain question:

- gives the bumper something to reveal
- links three separate conversations into a relay
- gives the audience a reason to watch the next segment.

It also produces good bumper material: a phone shot of the handwritten card, used as a collage element.

### Pre-chat checklist (per friend, 3 to 5 days before)
This is a 15-minute call or coffee, and it's separate from the generic checklist in 02.

- [ ] **Pitch the theme in one sentence** and the one-line hook. Ask whether they're in.
- [ ] **Agree the location** together, and check it against the five tests in Section 3.
- [ ] **Story mining:** "What are 2 or 3 stories you'd be happy to tell?" Note them, but **don't rehearse the answers**. Get the topic, not the telling. A retold story loses its life.
- [ ] **Off-limits list:** ask about exes, family, mental health, grades, other friends' business and anything involving people who haven't consented. Write it down and honour it.
- [ ] **Third parties:** any story that names a real, identifiable person who isn't present should be anonymised ("my roommate" rather than a name).
- [ ] **Review window:** offer them a look at their segment (or at least their clips) before publishing. With friends this protects the friendship, not just the content.
- [ ] **Release form** (template in 03). Send it now so it isn't a surprise on the day.
- [ ] **Wardrobe:** avoid fine stripes and big logos. Suggest a colour that fits the palette (the pink, green and yellow accents from 01), and make sure the three friends don't all wear black.
- [ ] **Logistics:** meeting time (30 minutes before recording, for setup and the off-mic catch-up), how to get there, and the backup location.
- [ ] **Tell them about the chain question** so they can think, but don't tell them who they're writing for.
- [ ] **Ask for 5 to 10 photos** of them with this place, or from the era it represents, for bumper collages. Get permission to use any photo that shows other people.

## 5. Question bank

### Core questions: ask all three friends, in this order, word for word

These follow the easy, personal, core, tension, reflection slope from 02, and they escalate the way Aron's sets do. Each is written to work across all four themes. Swap in the theme question at Q7.

1. **"We're sitting in ___. Why here? What's the first thing you remember about this place?"** This is the place opener, and it ties the location into the conversation.
2. **"How did we meet? Your version."** This gives the audience the relationship. Carolyn can add her version briefly.
3. **"What's taking up the most space in your head this week, the thing you'd think about in the shower?"**
4. **"Describe yourself the way your roommate (or mom, or sibling) would, not the way you would."**
5. **"What's something you were completely sure about at 15 that you've totally reversed on?"**
6. **"When was the last time you felt like you didn't belong somewhere? What did you do?"**
7. **The theme question:**
   - Theme A: "Who were you the first time you came here, and would that person like you now?"
   - Theme B: "What's in the folder you'd never let anyone open? You can describe the folder without opening it."
   - Theme C: "Make the case: why is this the best spot in the city, and what does it say about you that you think so?"
   - Theme D: the tense-shifted versions of "What are you becoming?"
8. **"What's a take you'd defend that most people here would disagree with?"** This gives the hot-take, SubwayTakes-style material, and it's very clippable.
9. **"What's something you think I get wrong about you?"** This turns the friendship into content without relying on inside jokes. It's the most intimate question, so ask it after the trust built in Q1 to Q8.
10. **"A year from today, what do you want to be true about your life?"**

**Closer (every friend):** "Write a question for the next guest. You don't know who it is." (the chain question). After that, ask 02's "Anything I should have asked but didn't?" *off* the formal list.

**Opener for Friends 2 and 3:** before Q1, they answer the chain question from the previous friend.

**Tips on the core bank:**
- Q3, Q5, Q8 and Q10 are the **best braid candidates**, because they're short and produce contrasting answers. Q7 is the backbone of the cold open.
- If time runs short, keep Q1, Q2, Q7, Q8, Q9 and the chain question. Drop Q4 and Q6 first.
- StoryCorps' published question lists are a good bank for more open, story-prompting questions, for example "Can you tell me about a moment when a person's kindness made a difference in your life?" ([StoryCorps Great Questions](https://storycorps.org/participate/great-questions/)).

### Friend-specific slots (3 to 5 questions each)
These go after Q6 and before Q7, so they land in the warmest part of the conversation. Build them from the pre-chat:

| Slot | Prompt to write the question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| S1: the story | One of the 2 or 3 stories they offered in the pre-chat | "Tell them about the night the fire alarm went off during your audition." |
| S2: the place | Something specific *in this location* | "What's that poster? When did it go up?" |
| S3: the expertise | What they know that Carolyn doesn't | "Teach me one thing about rowing that would make me sound like I know what I'm doing." |
| S4: the assumption | One of the three assumptions Carolyn wrote down about them | "I've always assumed you're never nervous. Is that true?" |
| S5: the wildcard | A playful question in the 73 Questions style, asked fast | "Most-used emoji? Last thing you bought? Worst class you've taken?" Fire 5 or 6 in a row. |

The S5 rapid-fire round works well because it's fast and fits a single shot. Vogue's 73 Questions is built on single-take rapid-fire questions in a place that belongs to the guest ([Daily Dot](https://dailydot.com/vogue-73-questions-webseries)). It also gives the edit a change of pace in the middle of each segment.

## 6. Editing approach

### The structure: linear segments with braided bookends
There are two ways to weave three conversations together:

- **Fully braided:** cut between all three friends question by question for the whole episode, as in Cut's "same question, many people" videos ([Seattle Met on Cut](https://www.seattlemet.com/arts-and-culture/2021/04/viral-cut-youtube-videos-based-in-seattle)). It's fast and comparative, but at 45 minutes it's exhausting. Viewers never settle into one relationship, and it is very hard to edit.
- **Linear:** three self-contained interviews in sequence, as in This American Life's theme-plus-acts structure, where a prologue sets up the theme and then the acts explore it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/This_American_Life)). This keeps each friendship intact, and the bumpers do the linking.

**Recommendation: mostly linear, braided at the start and end.** That means the braided cold open (0:00), three linear segments, and the braided finale (40:00). Viewers get the warmth of three real conversations plus the comparison payoff that makes this format different. Save the fully braided style for **short clips** (below).

### Workflow
1. **Transcribe everything** using the text-based editing in Descript or Premiere (03 covers access). Text-based editing turns a 40-minute interview into a document you can skim ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2023/10/16/premiere-pro-text-based-editing/)).
2. **Build a grid.** Rows are core questions Q1 to Q10 plus the chain question. Columns are Friend 1, 2 and 3. In each cell paste the strongest 1 or 2 sentences and the timecode, and star the standouts. This is a "paper edit" adapted for three guests. Documentary editors highlight transcripts and pick more than they'll use before touching the timeline ([NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/how-to-edit-an-interview-for-a-documentary/)).
3. **Read the grid across the rows.** The rows where the three answers *differ the most* become the cold open and the finale. Rows where all three agree are less useful for the braid, but can work as a "they all said the same thing" moment in the outro.
4. **Cut each linear segment** to about 12 minutes. Keep the full arc (opener, story, theme question, closer) and cut repeated points and logistics.
5. **Build the bumpers last.** By then you know which pull quote to recap and which tension to name in Bumper B.
6. **Record the outro voice-over after the rough cut**, so Carolyn reacts to what's actually in the episode.

### Making three locations feel like one show
- **Sound:**
  - Bring every segment to the same loudness target (03).
  - Lay a bed of room tone under each cut.
  - Use J-cuts and L-cuts (the next friend's audio starts under the end of the bumper) so the move between locations feels intentional rather than jarring ([NYFA](https://www.nyfa.edu/student-resources/how-to-edit-an-interview-for-a-documentary/)).
- **Picture:**
  - Apply one colour grade or LUT to all three segments, so a cold library and a warm dorm still look like the same show.
  - Use the same lower-third design (a desktop-dialog style) and the same caption font throughout.
- **Frames:** a recurring collage border or CRT-screen frame on the cold open and the braid helps tie the episode together. The braid in particular can show the three friends side by side in three desktop windows, like the two-Preview-windows pin on the board, which makes the comparison literally visible.
- **Music:** one theme sting for bumpers and one bed for the braid, the same in every episode. The royalty-free sources are in 03.

### Clips
This format produces a clip type that single-guest shows can't: **"I asked three friends the same question"**, in three vertical cuts, about 45 seconds total. Each episode should yield at least:

- one three-answer clip from the strongest row of the grid
- one solo story clip per friend
- one Q8 hot-take clip.

That's five or more clips per episode, enough for the cadence in 04.

**[Series note]:**
- Keep the grid template and the core questions, rotating only Q7. Over a season, the grid grows into a library of answers to the same questions, which allows episode-to-episode braids and a season-finale supercut.
- If it becomes a series, decide early between *three friends per episode* (as here) and *one friend per episode across a "three-location" season*. The first keeps the comparison hook but costs three shoots per episode. The second is far cheaper to produce, but you lose the cold-open trick.

| Show / video | What's similar | What to borrow |
|---|---|---|

| **Cut** ("100 People", "One Word", etc.) | Many people answer the same prompt, edited into a fast montage ([Seattle Met](https://www.seattlemet.com/arts-and-culture/2021/04/viral-cut-youtube-videos-based-in-seattle)) | The braided cold open, the finale and the three-friend clips |
| **Vogue's 73 Questions** | A single unbroken take of rapid-fire questions, filmed in a place personal to the guest ([Daily Dot](https://dailydot.com/vogue-73-questions-webseries)) | Location as personality, the S5 rapid-fire round, and a walking-tour opening for each location |
| **Comedians in Cars Getting Coffee** | A different car *and* café every episode, each chosen to match the guest ([Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/subcultures/comedians-in-cars-getting-coffee)) | The cleanest precedent for "the location is matched to the guest". It also opens each episode by explaining the choice, as Carolyn's Q1 does. |
| **SubwayTakes** | A fixed location, a single premise question and short episodes ([THR](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-features/kareem-rahma-interview-subway-takes-emmys-1236609132/)) | Theme C's premise. Also a reminder that one fixed ritual question makes a show recognisable. |

| **StoryCorps** | Two people who know each other, one asking the other big-life questions | The question bank, and the "friends interviewing friends" warmth ([StoryCorps](https://storycorps.org/participate/great-questions/)) |

## 8. Quick-start checklist

1. Pick a theme (B is the default) and write its one-line hook.
2. Pick three friends for contrast. Put a strong talker first or last.
3. Hold the pre-chats and agree locations. Scout each one at shoot time. Send permission emails, allowing 2 weeks for Cambridge public space.
4. Print the core question card and the chain-question index cards.
5. Shoot all three interviews within 7 to 10 days, with identical framing and mics. Record room tone, B-roll for the bumpers and a photo of the chain card.
6. Transcribe, build the friend-by-question grid, and pick the braid rows.
7. Cut the segments, then the bumpers, then record the outro. Export 5 or more clips.
