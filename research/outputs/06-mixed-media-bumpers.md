# Mixed-Media Bumpers: A Creative and Practical Guide for Carolyn

Prepared for Carolyn. This guide builds on [01-sample-analysis.md](01-sample-analysis.md), which covers her references (cosy two-person video podcasts, lyric videos, and Pinterest boards of collage, Y2K, retro-computer and CRT looks), and on [03-production-and-tools.md](03-production-and-tools.md), which covers gear, editing software, Harvard's Adobe access and music licensing. I don't repeat those here; I link back to them where they matter.

**The working assumption:** one episode made of three interviews with three friends, each filmed in a different place, joined by mixed-media bumpers. Where the advice changes if this becomes a series, it's marked **[Series]**.

---

## TL;DR

1. **Make three bumpers of 8 to 15 seconds each.** Bumper 1 opens the episode and introduces Friend 1. Bumpers 2 and 3 sit between the interviews and introduce Friends 2 and 3. Each one says: who is next, and where we are going.
2. **Build every bumper from physical objects collected at that friend's location**, such as receipts, tickets, leaves, wrappers and photos. Make a paper collage, animate it with a phone, and finish it on a fake desktop or a CRT TV.
3. **One template, three fillings.** Use the same frame (a Windows-95-style window), the same 4-colour palette, the same two fonts and the same 3-second sound signature in every bumper. Only the objects and the name change.
4. **Use free tools first:** Stop Motion Studio on a phone, then CapCut or DaVinci Resolve on the Mac. Move to Premiere or After Effects through Harvard's Creative Cloud only if you outgrow them.
5. **Never use a copyrighted song**, even as a joke "lyric video". Use your own voice, royalty-free music, or a sound you record yourself.
6. **Design at 16:9 but keep the important content in the centre square**, so each bumper can be cropped to 9:16 and posted as a teaser.

## 1. What a bumper is, and how long it should be

A **bumper** is a short piece that marks the line between two parts of a programme. On TV, it sits between the show and an ad break and identifies the show or channel. Wikipedia describes them as usually **2 to 15 seconds** long ([Wikipedia: Bumper (broadcasting)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bumper_(broadcasting))). In Carolyn's show there are no ad breaks, so the bumper does a different job: it is a **palate cleanser and a signpost** between interviews.

| Where | Example | Typical length | What to take from it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **TV: network IDs** | MTV's early-1980s IDs: stop-motion and mixed-media pieces that reveal the logo, such as "Club M", where the logo is built out of slices of ham, cheese and lettuce ([Fred Seibert](https://fredseibert.com/tagged/animation)) | About 5 to 15 s | **The closest match to Carolyn's idea.** It's the same logo every time, made from different stuff. Physical, handmade and funny. |
| **TV: Adult Swim "bumps"** | Black cards with white Helvetica text, a format that began in 2003. Fans have archived every bump in an online database called Bumpworthy ([Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/adult-swim-20th-anniversary-weird-bumpers/)) | About 5 to 20 s | One format that never changes makes the bumpers a recognisable "voice". It also proves a bumper can be just text. |
| **TV: anime eyecatches** | A still or short animation with the show's name, at the act break | **2 to 6 s** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bumper_(broadcasting)), [Eyecatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyecatcher)) | Short can be enough. One image plus a sound sting works. |
| **YouTube: bumper ads** | Google's non-skippable ad format | **6 s or shorter** ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2375464)) | Google sets the ad format at 6 seconds because that's enough for one idea. |
| **Podcasts: audio bumpers and stingers** | Short music pieces between segments. A *stinger* is a second or two; a *bumper* is about **5 to 15 s** and carries the show's name ([Storyblocks](https://www.storyblocks.com/resources/tutorials/what-are-bumpers-and-stingers), [Mubert](https://mubert.com/tools/cast/docs/what-is-stinger)) | 1 to 15 s | The sound matters as much as the picture (see section 6). |
| **Video podcasts: SubwayTakes** | Each episode is only 60 to 90 seconds, built around the opening question "So, what's your take?" ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/entertainment/how-comedian-kareem-rahmas-subwaytakes-became-youtubes-emmy-nominated-interview-sensation/2989169/)) | n/a | A repeated phrase and a repeated prop (the MetroCard mic) do the work of branding. Carolyn can repeat a phrase or prop in every bumper. |

**Recommended length for Carolyn:** **8 to 12 seconds**, with a hard ceiling of 15. That's long enough to show a location and a name, and short enough that people won't skip it. A 45-minute episode with three 10-second bumpers spends 30 seconds on them, which is under 2% of the running time. Also make a **3-second "mini bump"** version (just the logo and sound) for moments when an interview needs a breath.

## 2. Mixed-media techniques that fit her references

Difficulty: ★ = an afternoon, ★★ = a weekend, ★★★ = a real project.

### 2.1 Paper collage and scrapbook ★
- **The look:** torn edges, tape, magazine cut-outs and stickers on kraft or graph paper. It matches the torn-paper city collages and stitched-yarn portraits on her lyric-video board.
- **How to make it:** work on an A4 or US-letter sheet in **landscape** (matching 16:9). Glue down a background, but leave the pieces that will move loose. Photograph it from directly above in soft daylight; a window plus a white sheet of paper as a bounce is enough. Lock the phone's focus and exposure (long-press on iPhone) so the brightness doesn't flicker between frames.

### 2.2 Stop-motion with paper cut-outs ★★
- **The look:** jerky, handmade movement. Pieces slide in, names "type" themselves letter by letter, a photo flips over. This is the MTV "Club M" approach.
- **How to make it:** tape the phone to a stack of books or use a cheap overhead phone arm. Move each piece a few millimetres per frame. Shoot at **12 frames per second ("on twos")**, which reads as motion while keeping a handmade feel and halves the work ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/in/creativecloud/roc/blog/video/stop-motion-animation.html), [Animating on ones and twos](https://publish.obsidian.md/mister-chad/animation/animating+on+the+ones%2C+two%2C+and+threes)). **A 10-second bumper is about 120 photos.** Budget 1 to 2 hours of shooting per bumper.
- **Tips:** tack loose pieces down with Blu-Tack. Turn on onion-skinning in the app so you can see the previous frame. Don't touch the phone once you've started.

### 2.3 Scanned and photocopied textures ★
- **The look:** grainy black-and-white photocopies, risograph-style colour overlays in pink, green and yellow (from her board), and halftone dots.
- **How to make it:** photocopy her friends' photos at a library copier, ideally slightly too dark and copied twice to build grain. Scan the copies, or take flat phone photos of them. In the editor, put a **paper-texture scan on a top layer at 10 to 20% opacity with the "Multiply" blend mode** over everything, including the desktop graphics. That one layer makes digital and physical pieces look like the same object.
- **Make it once:** scan five sheets (blank paper, crumpled paper, a photocopy of nothing, graph paper and kraft paper) and reuse them across every bumper and every episode.

### 2.4 Handwriting and marker doodles ★
- **The look:** marker scribbles over black-and-white photos, like the hand-drawn words in "Where Are Ü Now" (from her lyric playlist).
- **How to make it:** for **live paper**, write the friend's name in marker one stroke at a time and photograph each stroke (stop-motion), or film it in real time and speed it up 4×. For **digital**, draw in Procreate over a still from the interview and export the drawing as a time-lapse or a PNG sequence. **Write the names yourself.** Her handwriting is a font no one else has.

### 2.5 Y2K and old Windows/Mac interface graphics ★★
- **The look:** grey Windows 95 dialog boxes, Paint, the Start bar, Mac folders arranged around a person. This is the biggest theme on both of her boards.
- **How to make it:** build the frames in **Canva or Keynote** using grey rectangles, a title bar and an "OK" button. Don't screenshot real Windows or Mac screens; build the look yourself so there's no question of using someone else's trademarked art. Example layouts:
  - A dialog box that reads `now_loading: [Friend 2].exe` with a progress bar that fills up.
  - A folder called `/locations/` that opens to show photos of the place.
  - A "Paint" window where her collage gets drawn on.
- In the editor, animate each window to "pop" in with a 2-frame scale-up. Leave the movement deliberately snappy rather than smooth.

### 2.6 Filming footage off a CRT TV ★★
- **The look:** scan lines, glow, curved glass and a slight colour fringe. It matches the "wall of CRTs" and the "subtitles on an old TV" pins.
- **How to make it:**
  1. Find a CRT TV. Try the Harvard Film Archive, the Lamont multimedia lab, campus freecycle lists, Facebook Marketplace or a thrift store (often $0 to $30). You also need a way to plug in: an **HDMI-to-RCA (composite) converter** costs about $15.
  2. Play the finished collage animation from a laptop through the converter.
  3. Film the screen with the phone on a tripod in a dark room. **Set the shutter to 1/60 (US, 60 Hz)** to cut down the rolling dark bars. Apps with manual shutter control, such as Blackmagic Camera (free) or ProCam, let you fine-tune it ([Jeff Geerling](https://www.jeffgeerling.com/blog/2025/recording-vintage-crts-modern-sony-mirrorless-camera/), [Retrobits CRT tips](https://theoasisbbs.com/crt-filming-tips-from-retrobits-for-better-retro-display-video/)). Watch for reflections on the glass and for the CRT's high-pitched whine in the audio; record sound separately.
  4. Film a little wider than the screen so the TV's plastic frame shows. The frame *is* the look.
- **No CRT?** Fake it with a free "CRT" or "VHS" effect in CapCut, or a scan-line overlay in DaVinci Resolve. It's acceptable, but it reads as a filter. A real CRT shot, even a single one, is far more convincing. **Reuse one real CRT shot** as the final frame of every bumper.

### 2.7 Photo-booth strips ★
- **The look:** four stacked frames, a white border, and goofy faces.
- **How to make it:** at each location, take a **4-shot burst of the friend** (Photo Booth on a Mac, a phone burst, or a real photo booth if there's one nearby). Print it, cut it into a strip and use it as a physical object in the collage. In the bumper, the strip can slide in and the frames can "flash" one at a time. Over the series, the strips become a collectable motif: one per guest.

### 2.8 Kinetic type, the lyric-video way ★★
- **The look:** words appear in sync with speech, each word slightly different (size, font, rotation), as in the lyric videos on her playlist.
- **How to make it:** pick **one line from the next friend's interview** (a teaser like "I've never told anyone this but…"). Put the audio on the timeline, then add a text layer for each word that appears exactly on the beat it's spoken. CapCut's auto-captions give word timings you can then restyle by hand. In After Effects, the simple route needs no plugins: one text layer per word, each with a quick scale keyframe.
- **Rule:** no more than **6 to 8 words** in a bumper. In a 10-second piece, more words than that stop reading as a design and start reading as subtitles.

## 3. Bumper concepts tied to the interview structure

The episode runs: **cold open → Bumper 1 (intro + Friend 1) → Interview 1 → Bumper 2 (Friend 2) → Interview 2 → Bumper 3 (Friend 3) → Interview 3 → outro**. Each concept below is a full *system*, so pick one and use it for all three bumpers.

### Concept A: "The Pocket Dump" (recommended)
At each location, ask the friend to empty their pockets or bag (or pick 5 things from the room). Photograph the objects flat, cut them out, and stop-motion them sliding into a scrapbook page that spells out the friend's name and place. It ends with the strip of four photo-booth frames and the show's logo stamp.
- **Why:** it's personal, it's specific to the place, and it doubles as a "what's in my bag" teaser for social.
- **Difficulty:** ★★.

### Concept B: "Loading Next Guest…"
A Windows-95 desktop. A folder named after the location opens (`/dunster_kitchen/`) and photos from the place tumble out as windows. A dialog pops up: `Connecting to [Friend]… OK`. Click OK, cut to the interview.
- **Why:** it uses her strongest visual theme, and it can be built mostly in Canva with no physical shooting.
- **Difficulty:** ★ to ★★.

### Concept C: "Channel Surf"
A CRT TV sits in Carolyn's room. She changes the channel with a remote, and each "channel" is a 1-second flash of the next location (street sign, door, view from the window), then the friend's face. It lands on the friend's channel with the name written in marker on masking tape stuck to the TV.
- **Why:** it's the most cinematic, it's filmed live rather than animated, and the TV becomes a recurring "set".
- **Difficulty:** ★★ (needs a CRT).

### Concept D: "Postcard From…"
Each bumper is a postcard: the front is a collage of the location, and it flips over to show the back, handwritten by the friend, with "wish you were here" and a teaser line. The stamp is the show's logo.
- **Why:** it puts the friend's own handwriting in it, and it's very quick (two photos plus one flip).
- **Difficulty:** ★.

### Concept E: "Say It Back" (kinetic type)
Black or kraft-paper background. One teaser line from the next interview plays, and each word lands as a cut-out magazine letter in ransom-note style, lyric-video timing. The location name is stamped underneath.
- **Why:** it's the purest lyric-video idea, and it gives a hook for the next segment.
- **Difficulty:** ★★. Pick the teaser line *after* editing the interview.

**Mix and match:** A plus B is a strong combination. The pocket-dump objects are scanned and then appear *inside* the desktop window, and the last frame is filmed off a CRT (C) as the shared ending of every bumper.

Target: **10 seconds at 24 fps** (stop-motion shot at 12 fps on twos). Format: 16:9 at 1920×1080, with key content inside the centre 1080×1080 square.

**Shot list to collect at the location (about 10 minutes of the shoot day):**
1. 5 objects from the room, photographed flat on a white sheet from above in daylight, one at a time (or take the objects home if the friend agrees).
2. A 4-shot photo-booth burst of the friend, framed chest-up, with plain background.
3. 10 seconds of room tone and 5 to 10 seconds of a location-specific sound (the kettle, the radiator, the street).
4. 3 "establishing" stills: the door or sign, a wide shot of the room, a detail.
5. One handwritten line from the friend on a sticky note or postcard (for Concept D or as a bonus).

## 4. Keeping the three bumpers consistent as a set

Think of it as a **template with three fillings**. The things that stay the same are what make the bumpers read as one show. The things that change are what make each guest feel special.

| Element | Stays the same in every bumper | Changes per friend |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | The same beats: open → objects arrive → name written → photo strip → dialog "OK" → CRT out | The objects and the location |
| **Recurring motif** | One "hero object" that appears every time, e.g. a **paper cut-out of a vintage microphone**, the show's logo stamp, or Carolyn's hand entering the frame | Which object it lands on |
| **Frame** | The same Windows-95 window, the same size and position | The folder name |
| **Colour palette** | 4 colours, taken from the reference boards: **kraft/cream paper `#EFE6D2`, ink black `#1A1A1A`, riso pink `#FF5FA2`, riso green `#3DBE8B`**, plus yellow `#FFD23F` as an accent. Each friend gets *one* of pink, green or yellow as "their" colour. | The accent colour (Friend 1 = pink, Friend 2 = green, Friend 3 = yellow) |
| **Typography** | **Two fonts only:** a pixel or system font for interface text (e.g. *VT323* or *Silkscreen*, both free on Google Fonts) and Carolyn's handwriting for names | The text itself |
| **Texture** | The same paper-scan overlay at the same opacity | — |
| **Sonic signature** | The same 2 to 3 second sound logo at the end of every bumper (see section 6) | A location sound before the logo |
| **Length** | 10 s, give or take a second | — |

**How to make it a real template:**
- In **CapCut**, finish Bumper 1 and then use *Duplicate project*. Swap the images on the same tracks.
- In **DaVinci Resolve**, make Bumper 1 a **compound clip**, duplicate it, and change the media inside.
- In **Premiere/After Effects**, build the window and the dialog box as a **Motion Graphics template (.mogrt)** with a text field for the name and a colour control for the accent colour.
- Keep a single folder `bumper-kit/` with the paper scans, the window graphics, the fonts, the sound logo and a one-page style sheet (this table). Anyone (including a future editor) can then make a new bumper.

**[Series]** In a series, the template becomes the show's *identity*. Keep the structure and sound frozen for at least a season. Change the accent colour or hero object by season, not by episode. Add a small episode number in the window's title bar (`ep_04`). Consider making the photo-booth strips into a recurring "wall" that fills up over the season, which also works as end-of-season social content.

### 5.2 Step-by-step: from physical collage to finished video

**Before the shoot day**
1. **Lock the template.** Write the one-page style sheet (palette, fonts, beats, length). Build the window, dialog box and logo stamp once in Canva. Scan 5 paper textures.
2. **Make the sound logo** (section 6) and render it as a WAV file.

**At each location (about 10 minutes, inside the interview shoot)**
3. Run the shot list from section 3: objects, photo-booth burst, room tone, location sound, establishing stills, the friend's handwriting.

**At the desk (about 3 to 4 hours per bumper the first time, about 1.5 hours once the template exists)**
4. **Print and cut.** Print the photos (a library printer works; slightly low-quality prints look *better*). Photocopy them if you want grain. Cut out the objects.
5. **Build the collage** on a landscape sheet. Mark the centre square lightly in pencil; anything important stays inside it.
6. **Set up the rig.** Phone overhead, mounted on books or an arm, daylight from one side, white card on the other. In Stop Motion Studio, lock focus and exposure and set **12 fps**.
7. **Shoot the stop-motion** beat by beat (objects arrive → name written → strip slides in). Shoot each beat as a separate clip; it's easier to retime in the edit.
8. **Export** from Stop Motion Studio as a video (or image sequence) and AirDrop it to the Mac.
9. **Composite in the editor** on a **1920×1080, 24 fps** timeline: desktop background → stop-motion clip inside the window → dialog box → paper-texture overlay (Multiply, 15%) on top.
10. **Add the sound:** room tone underneath, Foley sounds on each action, the sound logo at the end. Set the sound logo to the **same loudness as the voices** (about −16 LUFS for the whole episode, see [02-format-and-craft.md](02-format-and-craft.md)). Don't let the bumper be louder than the interview.
11. **CRT pass (optional, once):** play the finished bumper's last 2 seconds on the CRT, film it at 1/60 shutter, and cut that in as the ending. Reuse the same CRT ending clip in the other two bumpers.
12. **Export two versions:** the 16:9 master (1920×1080, H.264, high bitrate) for the episode, and a 9:16 version (1080×1920) for social (section 7).
13. **Template it:** duplicate the project and swap in Friend 2's material. Repeat for Friend 3.
14. **Drop into the episode** at the joins. Cut into each interview on the first word, not on a "so, um".

## 6. Sound for bumpers

### 6.1 A sonic signature
The sound is what tells people "this is a bumper" even when they aren't looking, which matters because many people listen to video podcasts with the screen in their pocket. Make a **2 to 3 second sound logo** and use it in every bumper and as the ending of every social clip.

**Ways to make one for free:**
- **Record it yourself.** Fit the Y2K theme with a combination of real sounds: a mouse double-click, a few seconds of a dial-up modem, a CRT TV power-on "thunk", a camera shutter. Then add a **3-note melody** played on GarageBand's built-in keyboard or a toy keyboard. Everything made this way belongs to Carolyn.
- **Ask a musician friend** for a 3-second sting, with written permission (a one-line email is enough).
- **Royalty-free:** pick a short "logo" or "stinger" track from the sources below and keep it as the sting forever.

### 6.2 Royalty-free music and sound effects

|---|---|---|---|

### 6.3 Why not a copyrighted song, even "lyric-video style"
The lyric-video references are tempting: one line of an Olivia Rodrigo song with kinetic type would look perfect. **Don't.**
- **YouTube's Content ID matches automatically**, and it matches both the **recording** and the **composition, meaning the melody and the lyrics**. So even singing it yourself, or typing out the lyrics over other music, can be claimed ([Uppbeat: Content ID explained](https://uppbeat.io/blog/youtube-content-id/), [Lickd](https://help.lickd.co/knowledge/youtubes-content-id)).
- A claim on a bumper is a claim on the **whole episode**. The rights holder can take its ad money, block it in some countries, or mute it. A 5-second clip can cost a 45-minute interview.
- Short doesn't mean safe. "Under 10 seconds is fine" and "I gave credit in the description" are myths; credit is not permission. Fair use is a legal defence decided case by case, not a rule that creators can count on (not legal advice).
- Podcast platforms (Spotify, Apple) have their own rules, and the audio version has no Content ID to "just" take the money; it can simply be removed.

**Do this instead:** keep the *look* of a lyric video and replace the *lyrics* with **her friends' own words** (Concept E). It's more original, and it previews the episode.

## 7. Specs: 16:9 episode, 9:16 clips

| Setting | Full episode (YouTube, Spotify video) | Vertical clips (Shorts, Reels, TikTok) |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspect ratio** | 16:9 | 9:16 |
| **Resolution** | 1920×1080 (shoot 4K if the phone can, to allow reframing) | 1080×1920 |
| **Frame rate** | **24 fps**: cinematic, and stop-motion at 12 fps doubles neatly into it. 30 fps is also fine. **Pick one and use it for every camera, every location and every bumper.** Mixing 24 and 30 causes stutter. | Same as the master |
| **Stop-motion** | Shot at 12 fps (each photo held for 2 frames) | Same |
| **Length** | Bumper 8 to 15 s inside a 30 to 50 min episode | Shorts up to 3 minutes; 20 to 40 s performs best ([Opus](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-shorts-dimensions), [PostFast](https://postfa.st/sizes/youtube/shorts)) |
| **Export** | H.264 or H.265 MP4, high bitrate | H.264 MP4 |

### Designing bumpers that double as social clips

1. **Centre-square rule.** Inside the 1920×1080 frame, keep the friend's name, the photo strip and the main objects inside the **centre 1080×1080 square**. The sides can hold decoration (extra stickers, desktop icons) that's fine to lose when cropping to 9:16.
2. **Mind the vertical safe zones.** On Reels, Meta reserves about **14% at the top, 35% at the bottom and 6% on each side** for the app's buttons and captions; on a 1080×1920 frame that leaves a safe area of about 950×980 in the middle ([Hopper HQ](https://www.hopperhq.com/blog/instagram-reel-size/), [Inro safe-zone checker](https://www.inro.social/tools/instagram-reels-safe-zone-checker)). Put a guide rectangle on a separate layer in the editor.
3. **Better still, rebuild rather than crop.** Because the bumper is made of layers (background, window, stop-motion, text), it takes about 15 minutes to re-arrange them on a 1080×1920 canvas: stack the window above the photo strip instead of side by side. Paper collage also works well tall, so consider shooting the stop-motion on a sheet with **a bit of extra space top and bottom**.
4. **Make each bumper a teaser.** For social: **bumper (10 s) + the best 15 to 30 s from that friend's interview + the sound logo**. The bumper works as a branded "cold open" for the clip, which matches the clip strategy in [04-launch-plan.md](04-launch-plan.md).
5. **Burn in captions** on the vertical version, using the same pixel font as the bumper, since many people watch with the sound off.
6. **First frame counts.** The first frame of the vertical version should already show something (the full collage or the friend's face), not the empty desktop. Vertical viewers decide in 1 to 2 seconds.

**[Series]** For a series, make a fixed 9:16 template alongside the 16:9 one, so every episode ships with three ready-made teasers. Use the same end card on all of them (sound logo + show name + "full episode on YouTube").

## Unresolved and caveats

- **Stop Motion Studio pricing:** the vendor's help page confirms a free version plus a one-time Pro upgrade, but doesn't list the current price or which features are paid; check in the App Store. One third-party source says there is no free Mac version.
- **CapCut's paywall keeps moving.** Check for the "Pro" badge on every effect or template before you build on it; the restructure noted here is from 2025–2026 reviews, not CapCut's own price page.
- **The YouTube Audio Library licence** for non-YouTube use (the audio podcast feed) is unclear in YouTube's own documentation. Treat standard-licence tracks as YouTube-only.
- **Harvard Adobe access** is covered and caveated in 03-production-and-tools.md.
- **Copyright notes here are general information, not legal advice.**
- **Naming the friend's location on screen:** if a location is a private room, ask each friend whether they're comfortable with it being identifiable, and keep addresses, room numbers and door numbers out of shot.
